## Situation

During the occupation of Kuwait (August 1990 to February 1991), Iraqi forces laid very large
numbers of mines along the Saudi border, the coast, power lines, highways and around the oil
fields. Coalition air-delivered scatterable mines and large quantities of unexploded
submunitions and other ordnance were added to them (Landmine Monitor, 1999). In the immediate
aftermath the contamination was estimated at **5–7 million mines over about 728 km²**. By late
1995, once the major clearance programmes had finished, the total actually found was about **1.7
million**. This is a textbook example of how early estimates of contamination can be wrong by a
factor of several (Landmine Monitor, 1999). Other sources give "an estimated 2 million" mines
laid and about 1.65 million removed to date (Wikipedia, *Kuwait Environmental Remediation
Program*, citing Al Jazeera).

Kuwait had money and an urgent economic need to restore its oil fields, coast and roads, but
almost no domestic clearance capacity. It decided to **buy** clearance at national scale.

## Technology available

| Capability | State in 1991–95 | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Hand-held metal detectors | Mature for metal-cased mines; weaker for low-metal types | Detection probability depends on the mine type and the soil ([05.2](lessons/stage-05/lesson-02.md)) |
| Mechanical systems (flails, tillers, sifters) | Available from military and commercial sources | Fast on sand, but the performance standards of the day did not cover them well (Landmine Monitor, 1999) |
| Mine detection dogs | Used by commercial contractors of the period | Likewise outside the UN standards of the time |
| Positioning | Early GPS (civil accuracy degraded until 2000) plus traditional survey | Recording boundaries and finds accurately was difficult |
| Information management | Paper, early databases; Kuwait later built a national environmental information system to plot recovered items | Data quality varied between sectors and contractors |
| Standards | The UN *International Standards for Humanitarian Mine Clearance Operations* were being developed; a **99.6% clearance** figure was used, partly to support commercial contracting (Landmine Monitor, 1999) | "Clear" was defined as a percentage, not as land that is safe for use |

## Information available to planners

**Known**

- Broad locations of minefields: the defensive belts were partly recorded, and the coalition
  knew where it had struck.
- The economic priority areas: oil fields, the coast, infrastructure.
- The budget, which was large by any humanitarian standard.

**Unknown**

- Total quantities (estimates ranged from about 1.7 million to 7 million).
- Exact boundaries of many minefields; scattered and unrecorded contamination.
- **Where mines had migrated.** Wind storms moved sand and buried mines deeper, so a surface
  that was clean on one inspection could hide a mine on the next (Landmine Monitor, 1999,
  citing US State Department *Hidden Killers* 1994).
- How reliable each contractor's work was.

## Hazards

- **Unexploded ordnance (UXO)** in very large quantities: submunitions, projectiles, rockets and
  bombs. Reporting indicates that most deminer deaths were caused by UXO rather than by mines
  (gulflink, DoD; see Sources).
- **Mines** of several types, some buried deep by drifting sand.
- **Environmental hazards**: oil lakes and contaminated soil from the oil-well fires, heat and
  sand.
- **Organisational hazards**: many contractors working to different procedures in adjacent
  areas, and the commercial pressure of fixed-price or time-limited contracts.

## Decisions

### Decision point 1 — how to organise national-scale clearance

| Known | Unknown |
|---|---|
| Urgency; money available; no domestic capacity | Contractor quality; true scale of contamination |

Kuwait **divided the country into sectors** and negotiated clearance contracts with coalition
nations. Reports name six initial sectors (United Kingdom, United States, France, Egypt,
Bangladesh, Pakistan), with a seventh later added for Turkey. Egypt, Bangladesh and Pakistan
used their own military personnel. The UK, US and France used commercial contractors. The UK,
for example, retained the firm Royal Ordnance, which employed former military EOD personnel
(gulflink, DoD, summarising US State Department *Hidden Killers* reporting). The allocation was
partly political: it rewarded coalition partners.

**Trade-off.** Sectorisation parallelises the work and assigns clear accountability for each
area. It also creates **heterogeneity**: different procedures, equipment, training, records and
incentives on either side of a sector boundary. That heterogeneity is exactly what quality
assurance then has to detect.

### Decision point 2 — what "cleared" means and how to verify it

| Known | Unknown |
|---|---|
| A contractual standard (a percentage) | The residual density actually left in any given area |

A 99.6% clearance rate sounds high. At national scale the arithmetic is sobering:
$1.6\times10^6 \times 0.004 = 6{,}400$ items potentially left behind, even if every sector met
the standard. Humanitarian agencies argued that a percentage standard is not enough for land
that people will use. The criterion must be *land fit for use with no known hazard remaining
to a specified depth*, verified by **independent quality assurance** (Landmine Monitor, 1999).

### Decision point 3 — what to do when QA fails

The reported outcome: all 728 km² received initial clearance, but **many minefields failed
quality-assurance inspection**. Uncleared mines were found, and large areas had to be
resurveyed and re-cleared (Landmine Monitor, 1999). The decision was between accepting residual
risk (marking and restricting land) and paying twice for the same area. Kuwait paid for
re-clearance in significant areas and still declared residual contamination years later.

## Technology used

Manual detection and excavation, and the mechanical and animal-based methods that commercial
contractors used at the time (Landmine Monitor, 1999), plus a growing national information
system to record items found. The Landmine
Monitor 1999 notes that Kuwait created an environmental information system to record and plot
mine and UXO locations.

## Outcome

| Metric | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Area given initial clearance | ~728 km² | Landmine Monitor 1999 |
| Mines and UXO cleared | > 1.6 million | Landmine Monitor 1999 |
| Expatriate deminers employed | ~4,000 | Landmine Monitor 1999 |
| Deminers killed | **84** (plus about 200 injured) | Landmine Monitor 1999 |
| Cost | USD 700 million (introduction) or USD 800 million (country chapter), both in the same report | Landmine Monitor 1999 |
| Cost per km² | ~USD 0.96 million (from 700M / 728 km²) | Landmine Monitor 1999 |
| Civilian mine casualties 1991–95 | 1,700 Kuwaiti civilians killed (as reported) | Landmine Monitor 1999, citing Roberts & Williams 1995 |
| Residual contamination | Reported as continuing decades later; mines still exposed by storms and rain | Wikipedia (KERP), Al Jazeera 2023 |

Two derived rates put the human cost in perspective: $84/4{,}000 \approx 2.1\%$ of the
expatriate workforce killed, and $84/728 \approx 0.12$ deaths per km² cleared.

## Lessons learned

1. **Estimates of contamination are priors, not facts.** Initial figures were 3–4 times the
   quantity actually found. Survey, not assumption, has to drive the plan. This is the
   foundation of land release ([05.7](lessons/stage-05/lesson-07.md)).
2. **A percentage standard does not measure safety.** What matters is the *residual hazard
   density* on the land when it is handed over, together with its uncertainty. Modern standards
   define clearance as removing all specified hazards to a specified depth, within an
   "all reasonable effort" framework (IMAS 07.11).
3. **Independent QA is the control loop of a contracted programme.** Without it the client
   cannot tell good work from bad. The Kuwait QA failures were found only because inspections
   happened.
4. **Heterogeneous contractors need common data standards.** Records of what was found, where
   and to what depth, in a common format, make QA, re-survey and later land use possible. That
   is the problem IMSMA-type systems later addressed.
5. **UXO, not only mines, kills deminers.** Planning, training and protection have to cover the
   whole range of explosive hazards.
6. **The environment changes the answer.** Mobile sand means that "clear" at time $t$ does not
   imply clear at $t+\Delta t$. Clearance is a state of the land that can decay.

<div class="callout key">

**QA as acceptance sampling.** Suppose QA inspectors re-search a random sample of area $a$
(km²) within a released block, with detection probability $p_{qa}$, and find **zero** items.
If residual items are Poisson with density $\lambda$ per km², then
$P(\text{0 finds}) = e^{-\lambda p_{qa} a}$. Setting this equal to 0.05 gives a one-sided 95%
upper bound $\lambda_{95} = -\ln(0.05)/(p_{qa}a) \approx 3/(p_{qa}a)$, the "rule of three".
With $a = 5$ km² and $p_{qa} = 0.9$: $\lambda_{95} \approx 0.67$ items/km². Zero finds therefore
do **not** prove the land is clear. They bound the residual density, and the bound is only as
tight as the sampled area is large. Real QA also stratifies samples by risk (sector boundaries,
drift areas, contractors with previous failures).

</div>

## Technological developments that followed

- **International Mine Action Standards (IMAS)**, which replaced the early UN standards. Clearance
  is now defined by removal of all specified hazards to a specified depth. **Land release**
  combines non-technical survey, technical survey and clearance, with "all reasonable effort"
  as the criterion (IMAS 07.11, edition 2, 2026; GICHD, *A Guide to Mine Action*, 2014).
- **Test and evaluation of detectors** under controlled blind trials (CEN CWA 14747-1:2003), so
  that detection probability and false-alarm rate are measured, not assumed
  ([05.1](lessons/stage-05/lesson-01.md)).
- **Quality management** as a formal part of mine action: accreditation, monitoring, and
  post-clearance inspection by sampling (GICHD, 2014).
- **Information management** (IMSMA) as the backbone of national programmes (GICHD, 2014).
- Decades later, Kuwait's **Kuwait Environmental Remediation Program** (KERP) combines soil
  remediation with residual ordnance removal, using commercial contractors (Wikipedia, *KERP*).

## Discussion questions

1. The pre-clearance estimate was 5–7 million mines; about 1.7 million were found. Give three
   reasons early estimates can be so far off, and explain how evidence-based survey reduces the
   error. How would you represent the estimate as a probability distribution rather than a
   single number?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer — then reveal</summary>

Reasons: estimates based on stockpiles or laying capacity rather than on evidence; double
counting across sources; assumptions about uniform density over whole areas; political or
funding incentives to present large numbers; inclusion of UXO or other items in "mine" counts.
Evidence-based survey replaces area-times-assumed-density with observed evidence points,
boundaries and technical-survey samples. As a distribution, you might use a log-normal or a
hierarchical model (density per polygon × area), updated by survey results. The initial wide
prior (millions to several millions) narrows as polygons are confirmed or cancelled.

</details>

2. Compute the expected number of residual items in a 10 km² block if the contract standard is
   99.6% and the block originally held 3,000 items. Then compute the 95% upper bound on residual
   density if a QA sample of 1 km² with $p_{qa}=0.85$ finds nothing. Are these two numbers
   consistent? What would you tell the landowner?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer — then reveal</summary>

Expected residual: $3{,}000\times0.004 = 12$ items, that is 1.2 items/km². QA bound:
$3/(0.85\times1)\approx3.5$ items/km². The zero-find QA result is **consistent** with the
contract-level residual (1.2 < 3.5), so this QA sample cannot tell the contractor apart from a
perfect one. To detect a residual density of about 1 item/km² with high confidence, QA would
need to sample several km² ($a \gtrsim 3/(p_{qa}\cdot1)\approx 3.5$ km²). The landowner should
be told that the land meets the contract standard but that residual items are expected, with a
realistic estimate and guidance on reporting finds.

</details>

3. Sectorisation by nation and contractor sped up the work but produced uneven quality. Design a
   QA sampling plan that allocates inspection effort across sectors. Which covariates would you
   use, and how would you update each contractor's sampling rate as results come in?

4. Most deminer deaths were attributed to UXO rather than mines. What does that imply for
   training, equipment and procedures in a programme that was contracted as "mine clearance"?

5. Wind-driven sand buried mines deeper after clearance. Model "clearance" as a state that decays
   over time. What monitoring or re-survey policy would you propose for drift-prone areas, and how
   would you decide its frequency?

<details class="answer"><summary>Answer — then reveal</summary>

One approach: model the probability that an item becomes undetectable (buried below detection
depth) or detectable (exposed at the surface) as a function of time and wind exposure. Designate
drift-prone polygons from terrain and wind data. Schedule re-survey there after major storms
(event-triggered) and at intervals chosen so that the expected number of newly exposed items
stays below a threshold. Combine this with reporting by land users. The frequency is set by
balancing survey cost against expected harm, using finds from each re-survey to update the rate.

</details>

6. Compare the Kuwait model (commercial contractors, fixed national sectors, high budget) with a
   humanitarian model built on national capacity ([CS-5](case-studies/cs05-laos-cluster-munitions.md)).
   Which risks does each model manage well, and which badly?
